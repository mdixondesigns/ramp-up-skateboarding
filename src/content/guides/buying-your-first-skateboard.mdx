<p class="standfirst">You don't need to buy anything to start — we lend boards at every clinic. But once your kid asks for their own, here is how to spend $90 well instead of $40 badly.</p>

The board sold next to the scooters at a big-box store looks like the board in the skate shop. It isn't. The wheels are hard plastic, the bearings are slow, and the trucks won't turn. A kid on that board pushes twice, stops dead, and decides skateboarding is not for them. That's not a talent problem — it's an equipment problem, and it's the single most common reason beginners quit.

## What a complete costs

A "complete" is a board that arrives assembled. From a real skate shop, a decent beginner complete runs $90–$140. That is the whole purchase — deck, trucks, wheels, bearings, grip tape. Below about $80 you are usually paying for a toy; above $150 you are paying for parts your kid won't notice for two years.

Shoe size matters more than age. If your kid is small for eleven, size down. And do not buy big "to grow into" — an oversized board is genuinely harder to control, and the deck will be worn out long before they've grown.

## Buy the helmet first

If the budget only stretches to one thing this month, make it a helmet. A certified skate helmet is $35–$60, fits your kid specifically, and is required at every Ramp Up session. Boards we can lend you. A helmet needs to fit one head.

Skip the used helmet from the neighbor. Foam compresses after a single real impact, and there is no way to see that from the outside. Wrist guards are the other high-value purchase — wrists take the fall in the first few months, and guards are around $20.

## Where to buy around Lancaster

Buy local if you can. A shop will size the board to your kid, set the trucks, and re-tighten them for free forever. That service is worth more than the twenty dollars you'd save online, and shop staff are usually thrilled to help a first-timer. If you do buy online, stick to skate-specific retailers rather than a general marketplace.

One more thing: budget nothing for the second board. Kids outgrow decks slowly, and the first one usually lasts two or three years of regular skating. Replace wheels and bearings before you replace anything else.
